STANDARD DEVIATION — LESSON PLAN
============================================================

A five-scene video study showing how higher σ means higher data instability.

SCENE 1: DATASET SPREAD
--------------------------------------------------
Duration : ~8 s
Concept  : Same mean, different spread
Video    : /home/user/carrot-crab/media/videos/std_dev_viz/480p15/DatasetIntroScene.mp4

SUMMARY
Three bar charts are shown side by side, all with the same mean but
progressively wider spreads: Stable (σ≈0.5), Moderate (σ≈2.5), Chaotic
(σ≈5.5). A dashed mean line runs through each.

TALKING POINTS
  1. Ask students: 'Do these three datasets feel the same to you?'
  2. Point out: the mean (average) is identical in all three — yet they look completely different.
  3. Introduce vocabulary: 'spread', 'dispersion', 'variation'.
  4. Question: 'How would you measure how spread out the data is?'

KEY QUESTION: If two factories report the same average output, can we trust them equally?

BRIDGE: Lead into Scene 2 — let's measure that spread mathematically.

============================================================

SCENE 2: DISPERSION FROM THE MEAN
--------------------------------------------------
Duration : ~13 s
Concept  : Deviation = distance from the mean
Video    : /home/user/carrot-crab/media/videos/std_dev_viz/480p15/DispersionScene.mp4

SUMMARY
Dots start at the mean (x̄) then animate outward to their actual
values. Arrows show the deviation of each point. Low σ dots barely
move; high σ dots fly to the edges of the screen.

TALKING POINTS
  1. Watch: every dot starts at the same place — the mean.
  2. Each dot travels a distance = (value − mean). That distance is the deviation.
  3. Low σ row: dots cluster tight. High σ row: dots scatter far.
  4. Key insight: we need ONE number that summarises all those distances.
  5. Why not just average the raw deviations? (They cancel out to 0!)

KEY QUESTION: If deviations always sum to zero, how do we stop them cancelling?

BRIDGE: Answer: square them — leads directly into Scene 3.

============================================================

SCENE 3: BUILDING THE FORMULA
--------------------------------------------------
Duration : ~15 s
Concept  : σ = √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / n )
Video    : /home/user/carrot-crab/media/videos/std_dev_viz/480p15/StdDevFormulaScene.mp4

SUMMARY
Four-step animated construction of the standard deviation formula:
dataset → mean → squared deviations → final σ formula with a live
numeric worked example (σ = 2.00).

TALKING POINTS
  1. Step 1: Start with raw data [2,4,4,4,5,5,7,9].
  2. Step 2: Calculate mean x̄ = 40/8 = 5.
  3. Step 3: Subtract mean from each value, then SQUARE — squaring makes all values positive and amplifies large deviations.
  4. Step 4: Average the squares → variance. Take the square root → back in original units → σ.
  5. Stress: the square root is what puts σ back in the same unit as the data (e.g. kg, €, °C).

KEY QUESTION: Why do we square the deviations instead of taking absolute values?

BRIDGE: Squaring has nice mathematical properties (differentiable everywhere) and punishes large deviations more. Now let's see what different σ values LOOK like.

============================================================

SCENE 4: Σ AND DATA INSTABILITY
--------------------------------------------------
Duration : ~13 s
Concept  : Higher σ = wider swings = less predictable
Video    : /home/user/carrot-crab/media/videos/std_dev_viz/480p15/StabilityCompareScene.mp4

SUMMARY
Side-by-side animated line graphs. Left: low σ=0.50 (flat, stable).
Right: high σ=5.68 (jagged, chaotic). Both have the same mean. Curly
braces mark the spread. Caption: Higher σ → Wider swings → Less
predictable.

TALKING POINTS
  1. This is the core visual — same mean, one line flat, one line wild.
  2. Low σ: you can predict the next value with confidence.
  3. High σ: the next value could be almost anywhere.
  4. Use analogy: a student with consistent 70% scores vs one swinging from 20% to 100%.
  5. Manufacturing: a machine with high σ produces many defective parts even if the average is 'on target'.

KEY QUESTION: In which scenario would you feel more comfortable making a decision based on the data?

BRIDGE: Let's see a real-world domain where this matters enormously — finance.

============================================================

SCENE 5: REAL-WORLD IMPACT
--------------------------------------------------
Duration : ~11 s
Concept  : σ as a measure of risk / volatility
Video    : /home/user/carrot-crab/media/videos/std_dev_viz/480p15/RealWorldScene.mp4

SUMMARY
Two simulated assets tracked over 30 time steps. Asset A (green,
σ≈0.9) trends steadily upward. Asset B (red, σ≈3.7) crashes and spikes
wildly — same average return. A callout box: Low σ = predictable, low
risk. High σ = unpredictable, high risk.

TALKING POINTS
  1. Both assets have the same average return — so which would YOU invest in?
  2. Asset A: calm, predictable growth. Asset B: could double or crash to zero.
  3. In finance, σ IS the definition of volatility / risk.
  4. Other domains: weather forecasting, quality control, medical test reliability, sports performance.
  5. Summary: σ quantifies how much we can TRUST the mean as a representative value.

KEY QUESTION: Name three fields outside finance where high standard deviation would be dangerous.

BRIDGE: Discussion answers could include: medicine (drug dosage consistency), aviation (component tolerances), climate science (extreme weather events).
